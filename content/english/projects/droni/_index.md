A hobby almost always starts from a shallow question and immediately becomes technical. Mine was the same as many others: *but how does that thing actually stay in the air?*

That question is where everything I did this year started: the exams, the choice of the drone, and the discovery that the most interesting part is not the flying, but **the preparation for it**.

## The boring part: the exams

Before you can fly anything at all, in Europe you need the licences. I got both of them, online and without spending anything:

- **A1/A3 in Luxembourg**, on EUROCONTROL's LearningZone platform: course and exam fully online, free of charge. Access lasts three months and the certificate is valid for five years across all EASA Member States.
- **A2 in Spain**, through AESA's electronic headquarters: a proctored online exam, also free, once you have enrolled in the A1/A3 exam.

They are the same exams you would sit in Italy, but I sat them in the countries where the free route is simpler. The result is identical: a certificate recognised across Europe. I wrote down the whole process in [how I got the licences](/blog/drone-licence-a1-a3-a2/).

## The interesting part: the drone

The slowest part was the choice itself. I was looking mostly at the **DJI Mini**, for their versatility, but the cost made me think twice: it is the kind of purchase that, in my case, would have meant a significant expense for a hobby I did not yet know how much I would like.

I waited for a promotion and got the **BetaFPV Cetus Pro**, which hit the mark: 33 grams, a contained list price, brushless, and above all the entry point into the world of **FPV** — the acronym stands for *first person view*, and it means the camera is your eye. You look through the drone.

It weighs almost nothing, has fully ducted props, and its batteries charge in minutes: it is an object designed to be used, not admired.

## The part that surprised me: the simulators

This is the discovery that changed how I approach the whole thing. Before flying anything at all, I started **experimenting in the simulator**: FeelFPV TinyWhoop on Android and Liftoff on PC, which I covered in [a dedicated article](/blog/fpv-simulators/).

The key point is that **the drone's radio controller also works with the simulators**. You plug the transmitter in, open Liftoff, and you are flying — with the very same joystick you will use outside. That changes the nature of the learning: you are not playing a video game, you are training the hand that will later have to fly the real thing.

## How I will use the time

I don't have a fixed plan yet, and that is fine. What I do know is the order of things: first at home, then outdoors in simple conditions, and only after that something more demanding. The garage has been the first test bench, and it is the only place where I feel free to make mistakes.

It is a project, in the full sense of the word: not a product, not a career goal. Just new ground on which to learn something that has nothing to do with what I do for a living — and for me, that is half of the reason I chose it.
