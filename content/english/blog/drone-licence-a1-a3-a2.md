Since the European regulation came into force, flying a drone in Europe requires a licence. The question everyone kept asking me was always the same: **do you really have to take an in-person course?** The short answer is no. There are routes that are **fully online and free**, and I used both of them.

This is the route I followed.

## What A1/A3 and A2 actually are

First, two lines on the regulatory framework, because the acronyms are often used loosely.

The European Union has split operations into three categories: **Open**, **Specific** and **Certified**. Almost all drones fall under the Open category, itself divided into operational subcategories tied to **weight and class label**:

| Licence | What it allows |
|---|---|
| **A1/A3** | The entry level. Drones under 250 g (C0 label) with no particular restriction; C1 drones (up to 900 g) in A1 without flying over uninvolved people; C3/C4 drones or unlabelled ones up to 25 kg in A3, away from urban areas |
| **A2** | The advanced level. C2 drones (from 900 g to 4 kg) allowing you to get closer to uninvolved people while keeping safety distances (30 m, reducible to 5 m in low-speed mode) |

In practice: **A1/A3 is the entry door**, and for the vast majority of hobby drones that is all you need. **A2** is what you want when the drone is heavier and you need to get a little closer to people.

## Why I did them abroad

In Italy, ENAC handles both the training and the exam, and there is an online route there too, but with a small fee. In other European countries the very same exam is **free and with unlimited attempts**. Since the certificate is valid in all EASA Member States, the authority that issues it changes the language of the diploma but not its value.

## A1/A3: the Luxembourg route

Luxembourg offers the online A1/A3 theory exam through **EUROCONTROL's LearningZone platform**.

The features, according to the Directorate of Civil Aviation:

- course and exam **fully online**;
- available in **English, French, German and Luxembourgish**;
- **registration is free** and access remains valid for **3 months** (renewable);
- the certificate is **valid in all EASA Member States**.

Once you complete the training modules and pass the exam, you receive the **Remote Pilot Certificate** directly through the LearningZone platform. The certificate is valid for **5 years**.

Two details that are often taken for granted and actually are not:

- the A1/A3 exam is **mandatory** for UAVs between 250 g and 25 kg, and only *recommended* for those under 250 g;
- it is not an operational authorisation: it is proof that you completed the training.

## A2: the Spain route

For A2 the requirements are stricter, and this is true across Europe:

- you must already hold a **valid A1/A3**;
- you must have completed **practical training**, which can also be **self-training** (a school is not mandatory).

The A2 exam, unlike A1/A3, takes place in a proctored online environment: you need a **webcam, a microphone and screen sharing access**.

I did it through **AESA** (Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea), whose electronic headquarters makes both the theoretical training course and the exam available online, **at no cost**. The A2 exam, once passed, gives access to the A2 (and A3) subcategories of the open category.

## Operational requirements recap

Getting the licence is not by itself enough to fly anywhere. These always apply:

- **registration as a UAS operator**, with the operator number visibly marked on the drone;
- **120 m** maximum height;
- no flying over uninvolved people or assemblies;
- **civil liability insurance** (mandatory from A2 upwards);
- checking the **UAS geographical zones** before every flight.

## What this route taught me

If I had to start over, three things I learned:

1. **The exam is more accessible than it looks.** The fear of the bureaucratic step is almost always bigger than the exam itself. An hour of training and some attention are enough.
2. **You can get the very same certificate in several countries.** EASA standardises both the syllabus and the certificate: the issuing body changes, the value does not.
3. **The regulatory basics matter more than they seem.** Knowing the limits (120 m, distances from people, overflight) is what saves you when you show up somewhere uncontrolled with no map at hand.

So, my advice: do it online and do it for free. It is the cheapest and fastest way to remove the doubt and get to the fun part.
