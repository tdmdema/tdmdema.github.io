After months of reading up and the licence in my pocket, the real decision arrived: **which drone to buy**. The answer was not immediate, and the reason is more interesting than the drone itself.

![BetaFPV Cetus Pro](/images/blog/droni/cetus-pro.jpg)

## The dilemma: DJI Mini or entry-level FPV

My first instinct, as I imagine happens to a great many people, was the **DJI Mini**. They are excellent products: stabilisation, GPS, automatic return to home, a decent camera, good battery life. And they are very easy to fly, even if you have never held a radio controller.

But the cost made me think twice. A DJI Mini is a serious investment for a hobby that, let's be honest, I did not yet know how much I would like. And there was a second, deeper reason: those drones are designed to **give you footage**, not to let you **learn to fly**.

I wanted the opposite. I wanted a drone you have to *learn* to fly.

## Why I chose the Cetus Pro

After a few months of indecision I waited for a promotion and took the opportunity on a **BetaFPV Cetus Pro**. It sits exactly at the midpoint I was looking for:

| Specification | Cetus Pro |
|---|---|
| Takeoff weight | 33 g (without battery) |
| Power | 1S, BT2.0 450 mAh battery |
| Motors | 1102-18000KV brushless |
| Props | 40 mm tri-blade |
| Flight time | 4-5 minutes |
| Range | ~80 m |
| Receiver | Frsky D8 |
| Radio controller | LiteRadio 2 SE |

What convinced me is that it is a true **micro FPV**: light, brushless, with props fully ducted by the whoop frame — you can crash it into anything without destroying it.

The downsides, stated honestly, are real and worth stating:

- **4-5 minutes of flight time** per battery is a genuine limit, not a marketing figure. You learn to plan, but at the start it is frustrating.
- **Limited range** and no automatic return: if you lose it, it is gone. In that case the safety mode makes it land in an emergency when it loses signal or runs low on battery.
- **No GPS and no altitude hold**: in *angle* mode the drone tilts and goes, which is exactly what you want to learn.
- The camera is meant for FPV, not for video: you see it in real time, and that is all.

## The flight modes: N, S, M

This is the detail that made me realise how well designed an entry-level drone can be. The Cetus Pro has three modes, built as a progression:

- **N (Normal)** — the drone self-stabilises its angle and holds a hover. This is the mode for first contact, for getting familiar with the controls without fear.
- **S (Sport)** — stabilisation is reduced, the drone is more responsive and tilts further. This is the mode where you start to realise you are *piloting*.
- **M (Manual / Acro)** — stabilisation off. From here on, every movement is yours. This is how you really fly, and it is the goal.

There is also **Turtle mode**: flip the drone over and it rights itself. It sounds like a detail, but with a 33 g whoop it saves your day more than once.

## The radio controller: the discovery that changes everything

The **LiteRadio 2 SE**, the controller that ships with the drone, is not a toy remote. It is a real FPV transmitter, from the same family as professional radio controllers.

And here is the point that turned out to be the most important discovery for me: **it also works with the simulators**, on both PC and Android.

Plug it into the computer over USB and it behaves as a **USB joystick**: open Liftoff (or any other FPV simulator) and you are flying with the exact same controller you will use outdoors. This holds for the most widespread simulators — Liftoff, VelociDrone, DRL and others.

Why does it matter? Because it changes the nature of the learning. You are not playing a video game with a keyboard: you are **training the hand** that will later hold the real drone. The muscle memory you build in the simulator is the same one you will use outside. If you have ever read FPV flying advice, this is *the* piece of advice repeated everywhere: **simulate before you fly**.

## How I got on in my first tests

Flown twice, both times in my garage, with all the caution in the world. I do not yet have a formed opinion on the drone, because two flights are not enough, but I understood one thing that needs saying right away: **the difficulty is not the drone, it is the manual dexterity**.

The drone is robust and forgives a lot. The problem is my thumbs, which do exactly what they please. It is precisely the kind of problem that gets solved with hours in a simulator — and that is the direction I am heading, before trying anything outdoors again.

## Some advice for beginners

If I had to start over today, I would say three things:

1. **Do not buy the prettiest drone first, buy the drone that makes you progress.** An entry-level FPV with stabilisation in Normal mode is a great first step precisely because it lets you make mistakes.
2. **Invest in batteries.** With 4-5 minutes per charge, spare batteries are what turns a training session into a useful one.
3. **The simulator is the biggest investment you will make.** It costs very little and saves you dozens of crashes — both in the literal sense and in your wallet.

That is all for now: two garage flights, three batteries charged and a controller plugged into a PC. The next step is [some serious simulator time](/blog/fpv-simulators/), and then we will see.
